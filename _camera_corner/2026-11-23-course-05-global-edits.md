Lightroom lets you move any slider in any order, but some steps change what the later ones do. Working in a steady order gives better results and fewer second guesses.

Every edit here is non-destructive. Nothing touches your original file, and the **History** panel on the left lets you step back to any point.

## Getting into Develop

1. In the **Library** grid, click the photograph you want to edit
2. Press **D**, or click **Develop** at the top right
3. The editing panels are on the right. Right-click the word **Basic** and tick **Solo Mode**, so only one panel is open at a time

## 1. Foundations

### Profile

At the top of the **Basic** panel is **Profile**, usually set to Adobe Color. Click the menu to change it. **Adobe Color** is a good start. To see more, click the small **four-squares icon** beside it: hover over each profile to preview it, click to choose, then click **Close**. For RAW files, the **Camera Matching** group gives the look of the camera's own JPEG.

### Lens corrections

1. Scroll down the right side and click **Lens Corrections**
2. Tick **Remove Chromatic Aberration** (it removes coloured fringes on edges)
3. Tick **Use Profile Corrections**. Lightroom finds your lens and fixes distortion and darkened corners. If it cannot find the lens, choose the make and model from the menus underneath

### Crop and straighten

1. Press **R**, or click the **Crop** icon (a dotted square) under the histogram
2. Drag a corner or edge of the frame inward. Drag inside the frame to move the photograph
3. To straighten, drag the **Angle** slider, or click **Auto** to let Lightroom level the horizon
4. Click the **padlock** to lock or unlock the shape (or press **A**). **X** switches between landscape and portrait
5. Press **Enter**, or click **Close**, to apply

The Cropping and aspect ratios lesson covers composition.

## 2. Tones (Basic panel, top to bottom)

### White balance

1. In the **Basic** panel, click the **eyedropper** beside "WB" (or press **W**)
2. Move it over the photograph and click on something that should be neutral grey or white, such as a cloud, a wall or a road
3. If the result is still off, drag **Temp** (left for cooler and bluer, right for warmer and yellower) and **Tint** (left for greener, right for more magenta)

The White balance lesson explains why it matters.

### Exposure to Blacks

Work down the sliders in order, watching the photograph, not the numbers:

- **Exposure:** the overall brightness. Judge it on the main subject
- **Contrast:** a little often helps RAW files, which start flat
- **Highlights:** drag left to bring back detail in a bright sky
- **Shadows:** drag right to open up dark areas. Go gently, or the photograph turns grey and flat
- **Whites** and **Blacks:** set the brightest and darkest points

### Checking for clipping

"Clipping" means areas that have turned pure white or pure black, with no detail left.

- Hold **Alt** (Option on a Mac) while you drag **Whites**. The photograph turns black; anything that appears in colour or white is clipping. Stop just before large areas appear. Do the same with **Blacks**: the photograph turns white and clipped areas show dark
- Or press **J** to switch on clipping warnings: clipped highlights show red, clipped shadows show blue. Press **J** again to turn them off. You can also click the small triangles at the top corners of the histogram

The Black and white points lesson explains this in more detail.

## 3. Presence and colour

Still in **Basic**, under the tone sliders:

- **Texture** sharpens fine detail; **Clarity** adds mid-tone punch; **Dehaze** cuts haze. All three go wrong quickly, so keep them low, around +5 to +20
- **Vibrance** before **Saturation.** Vibrance lifts muted colours and protects skin tones

Then the panels below Basic:

- **Tone Curve:** for finer contrast. Switch to the **point curve** (one of the small round buttons at the top of the panel; hover to see their names), click on the line a quarter of the way up and drag down slightly, then three quarters of the way up and drag up slightly. That gentle S shape adds contrast
- **Color Mixer:** change one colour at a time. Choose the **Luminance** controls (in the HSL view), then click the small **target icon** (the Targeted Adjustment tool). Click on a blue sky in the photograph and drag downward to darken just the blues
- **Color Grading:** tint the shadows, mid-tones and highlights. Drag the small dot in a colour wheel outward a little. A little goes a long way. The Colour theory lesson helps here

## 4. Detail and finishing

### Sharpening and noise

1. Press **Z** to zoom to 100% on an important area
2. Open the **Detail** panel
3. **Sharpening:** the default (Amount 40) is a sound start. Hold **Alt** (Option) while dragging **Masking** to the right: white areas get sharpened, black areas are left alone. This keeps skies smooth
4. **Noise Reduction:** for high ISO photographs, click **Denoise**, check the preview and click **Enhance**. Depending on your version, it may save the result as a separate DNG file next to the original

The Sharpening and noise lesson covers the settings.

### Effects

In the **Effects** panel, a **Post-Crop Vignetting** Amount of around −10 gently darkens the corners. **Grain** adds film-like texture. Both are optional.

## Checking your work

- Press **\\** (backslash) to see the before version; press it again to return to your edit
- Press **Y** to see them side by side. Press **Y** again to go back
- Step away for a few minutes, then look again with fresh eyes

## Copying the edit to similar photographs

1. In the filmstrip, click the photograph you have edited
2. **Ctrl-click** (Cmd-click) the others you want to match
3. Click **Sync** at the bottom right of the panels
4. Tick the settings to copy. Usually leave **Crop** and **Masking** unticked. Click **Synchronize**

> Fix it, then finish it. Foundations first, flourishes last.
