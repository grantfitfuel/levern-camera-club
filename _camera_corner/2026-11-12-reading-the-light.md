Good landscape photographs are mostly about light. The subject matters less than you think: the same view can be flat at noon and extraordinary an hour after sunrise.

The light is also why I go out at all. Watching it build and change is when I lose track of time completely, and for me that is half the point of photography.

## Four things to read

Before you look for a subject, look at the light and work out four things.

- **Direction.** Where it comes from, relative to you and the subject
- **Quality.** Hard, with crisp shadows, or soft, with gentle ones
- **Colour.** Warm, cool or neutral
- **Contrast.** How far apart the brightest and darkest parts are

## Direction

- **Front light,** with the sun behind you, shows colour and detail but flattens shape, because the shadows fall behind the subject where you cannot see them
- **Side light** rakes across the surface. Every ridge, stone and ripple throws a shadow, so texture and depth appear
- **Back light,** with the sun in front of you, puts a rim of light round edges, makes leaves and grass glow, and turns solid shapes into silhouettes

Walk around the subject before you set up. A few steps can turn front light into side light, and change the picture with it.

## Hard and soft light

Hard light comes from a small source, such as the sun in a clear sky. It casts dark shadows with sharp edges. Soft light comes from a large source, such as a sky full of cloud, and wraps round the subject with gentle shadows.

Neither is better. Hard light suits strong shapes and graphic pictures. Soft light suits detail and colour. Midday sun is hard and comes from overhead, so shadows sit short and black under everything. That is why a view so often looks flat at noon.

## Starting settings

- **Mode:** Aperture priority; manual at blue hour, when exposures run to several seconds
- **Aperture:** f/8 to f/11, for sharpness from front to back in a landscape
- **Shutter speed:** 1/100 s to 1/800 s in daylight and golden hour; several seconds at blue hour, on a tripod
- **ISO:** Lowest native ISO, usually 100
- **White balance:** Daylight, so the warmth of golden hour stays; shoot RAW to adjust it later

These are starting points. Take a frame, check the histogram, and adjust one thing at a time.

## Golden hour

The hour after sunrise and the hour before sunset. The sun is low, so light comes in from the side, warm and soft, and every ridge and wall throws a long shadow. Texture appears that simply is not there at midday.

The further north you go, the more the timing swings with the seasons. In midsummer the golden light can last for hours. In midwinter the whole short day can feel like golden hour.

## Blue hour

The time just after sunset and just before sunrise, when the sun is below the horizon. The sky turns deep blue, street lights and windows come on, and towns look their best. Bring a tripod, because exposures run to several seconds.

## Overcast

Cloud works like a huge softbox. Shadows vanish and colours deepen, which suits woodland, waterfalls and close detail. When the sky is a flat white lid, leave it out of the frame.

## Weather is the light

Some of the best light arrives either side of rain. A break in the cloud after a squall can spotlight one hillside while everything around it stays dark.

- Look for clearing showers in the forecast, not clear skies
- Arrive early and be prepared to wait
- Keep shooting as the weather moves through

## Colour and white balance

Light has a colour. Golden hour is warm. Shade, blue hour and overcast days are cooler. Auto white balance tries to make everything neutral, so it can take the warmth out of a sunrise.

Set the white balance to Daylight and the colour you saw stays in the picture. Shoot RAW and you can change it afterwards in Lightroom without any loss of quality.

## Reading the light in the field

1. Arrive before the light you want, and look before you set up
2. Find the sun, or where it will be, and note its direction relative to the main view
3. Look at the shadows. Their length tells you how low the sun is; their edges tell you how hard the light is
4. Half close your eyes. Detail drops away and you see the big shapes of light and dark
5. Walk round the subject to try front, side and back light
6. Take a test frame and check the histogram
7. Keep shooting as the light changes. The best frame often comes a few minutes before or after the moment you planned for

## Worked scenarios

- **A hill farm on a clear day.** At noon the light is overhead and the view looks flat. Come back an hour after sunrise, stand with the sun at one shoulder, and the side light picks out every wall and ridge
- **A coastline on a grey day.** The sky is a flat white lid. Point the camera down, fill the frame with rock and water, and let the soft light carry the colour and detail
- **A showery afternoon.** Watch the cloud. When a gap lets the sun onto one hillside while the rest stays dark, expose for the lit area and let the rest fall into shadow
- **A town at blue hour.** Shortly after sunset the sky and the lit windows come close in brightness. On a tripod at f/8 and ISO 100, expect exposures of a few seconds

## Checking your result in camera

- **Back light** often blows the sky. Turn on highlight warnings, expose for the bright areas and let the shadows go dark, or bracket
- **High contrast** shows as a histogram touching both edges at once. The scene holds more range than one frame can record. Bracket, or wait for softer light
- **Colour** on the screen depends on your white balance setting. If a warm scene looks grey, check the setting before you blame the light

## Common mistakes

- **Arriving as the light peaks.** By the time you are set up, it has gone. Be in place half an hour early
- **Leaving at sunset.** The colour in the clouds often comes after the sun has gone, and blue hour follows
- **Shooting into the sun without shade.** Flare washes out contrast. Use a lens hood, or shade the lens with your hand
- **Keeping a dull sky in the frame.** On a grey day, crop it out
- **Letting auto white balance cool a warm scene.** Use Daylight, or correct it in the edit

> Bad weather makes good photographs. Bad light does not.
