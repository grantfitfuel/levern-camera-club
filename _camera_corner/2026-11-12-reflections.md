A reflection gives you the same subject twice, once upside down and slightly changed. The second version is darker, softer and often more interesting than the first.

## Where reflections hide

- **Still water.** Lakes, canals and harbours. Sheltered water, such as a dock or an enclosed pool, stays calm when open water does not
- **Puddles.** A puddle a metre across can hold a whole building if you get low enough
- **Wet sand.** A wave drawing back leaves a thin film of water that mirrors the sky for a few seconds
- **Glass.** Office blocks and shop windows reflect the street. A shop window also shows what is inside, so you get two layers
- **Metal.** Car bodywork and polished steel bend the reflection with their curves

## Starting settings

- **Mode:** Aperture priority, exposing for the brighter half, usually the sky; the reflection sits about a stop darker
- **Aperture:** f/11 to f/13, to hold a near puddle edge and a distant reflection both sharp
- **Shutter speed:** 1/100 s or faster handheld and on wet sand; several seconds on a tripod to smooth ripples
- **ISO:** 100 to 800; raise it only to keep 1/100 s in dull light
- **Focal length:** 24 mm to 35 mm full frame, the wider end for puddles so they fill the foreground
- **Focus:** On the reflected subject, not the surface; about 1.75 m at 24 mm and f/11
- **Filter:** Polariser off, or rotated to the position that keeps the reflection at full strength

These are starting points. Take a frame, check the histogram, and adjust one thing at a time.

## Where to put the line

The line where the scene meets its reflection works like a horizon. See the Where to put the horizon lesson for the general rule.

- **Halfway** gives you symmetry. Use it when the reflection is close to perfect and the subject can carry a formal, balanced picture. See the Symmetry and patterns lesson
- **Low in the frame** gives the scene the weight. Use it when the water is rippled or the sky is the best part
- **High in the frame** gives the reflection the weight. Use it when the reflection is cleaner than the real thing, or the scene above is cluttered

Whichever you choose, keep the line level. A tilted waterline is easy to spot, because the reflection tilts with it.

## Why the reflection is darker

Water reflects only part of the light that falls on it. The rest passes into the water or is absorbed. Looking steeply down, you see little reflection and a lot of what is under the surface. Looking across at a shallow angle, the reflection gets much stronger.

So a reflection is almost always darker than the scene, often by around a stop. That is useful: it tells the viewer which half is real.

- Expose for the brighter half, usually the sky
- If the gap is too wide, a soft graduated ND filter over the top half evens it out
- In editing, lift the reflection a little, but not to match. A reflection as bright as the scene looks fake

## Get low

The lower the camera, the shallower the angle to the surface, and the bigger the reflection becomes. From standing height, a small puddle shows a patch of sky. Put the camera a few centimetres above it and the same puddle can hold a whole building.

1. Find a puddle with something tall beyond it, and stand so the subject is directly behind it
2. Lower the camera until the lens is just above the water. A tilting screen saves lying in it
3. Shuffle sideways and forwards until the reflected subject sits inside the puddle, not across its edge
4. Use a wide lens so the puddle fills the foreground

## Wind and timing

The smallest breeze breaks a reflection into ripples. Wind is often lightest around dawn, so a calm early morning is the most reliable time for a mirror finish. A sheltered spot helps at any hour.

Wind comes in gusts, and the water can settle for a few seconds between them. Compose, then wait with your finger on the button. On wet sand, shoot just after a wave draws back.

If the water will not settle, a long exposure of several seconds averages the ripples into a smooth surface, and the reflection returns as a soft glow. See the Long exposure lesson.

## Polarisers

A polariser removes reflections, so it can ruin a reflection picture.

- Light reflected off water and glass is partly polarised, so a polariser cuts it. The effect is strongest when you look at the surface at roughly 35 to 40 degrees
- Rotate the filter while you watch the screen. One position kills the reflection; a quarter turn from it keeps it at full strength. Or take the filter off
- Reflections in bare metal are barely polarised, so a polariser does little to them

See the Filters lesson for how a polariser works.

## Focusing on the reflection

The reflection is not on the surface of the water. A mirror image sits as far behind the surface as the subject is in front of it, so to the lens a reflected building is roughly as far away as the real one.

- Focus on the reflection, or on the real subject, which is at much the same distance
- Focus on a leaf, a ripple or the edge of the puddle and the reflection will be soft
- Autofocus often grabs the surface. Use a single small focus point on a high-contrast edge in the reflection, or focus manually with the screen magnified

## Depth of field

Often you want the puddle edge and the reflected building both sharp. They are at very different distances, so you need depth of field.

A worked example, on full frame. At 24 mm and f/11, the hyperfocal distance is about 24 × 24 ÷ (11 × 0.03) = 576 ÷ 0.33, which is about 1.75 m. Focus there and everything from about 0.9 m to infinity is acceptably sharp. A puddle edge 1 m away and a building 50 m away will both hold.

If the puddle edge is nearer than that, you will have to choose. A soft puddle edge with a sharp reflection usually looks better than the other way round.

## Worked scenarios

- **A city puddle after rain.** Camera almost on the ground, wide lens, f/11, focus on the building beyond. Wait for the last raindrop ripples to die away
- **A lake at dawn.** Arrive before sunrise, polariser off. Put the line halfway if the reflection is perfect. If a breeze starts, move the line lower and let the scene take the weight
- **Wet sand.** Stand where the waves just reach and shoot a burst each time one draws back. 1/100 s or faster keeps the edge of the retreating water crisp
- **A glass tower.** Step sideways until the reflected building sits in a clean run of glass, not across a joint

## Turn it upside down

A reflection on its own, with ripples breaking straight lines into wobbling shapes, makes a strong abstract. Flip it upside down afterwards (Lightroom's Flip Vertical does it) and it reads the right way up but strangely painted. See the Abstract lesson.

## Common mistakes

- **Polariser left on.** The reflection fades. Take it off or rotate it
- **Focused on the surface.** Focus on the reflected subject instead
- **A tilted waterline.** Use the camera's electronic level. Straightening later loses the edges of the frame
- **Camera too high.** Get lower
- **Line placed by habit.** Give the stronger half more room, or commit to the middle
- **Your own reflection** in glass. Change your angle

## Checking in camera

1. Magnify the screen on the reflection and check it is sharp
2. Check the waterline against the level or the grid
3. Check the histogram: the sky should not press against the right edge, and the reflection should hold shadow detail
4. Scan the surface for ripples, leaves and litter

> Look down. The best picture of a building is sometimes in the puddle at your feet.
