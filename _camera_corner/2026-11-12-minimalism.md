Minimalist photographs strip a scene down to as little as possible: usually one subject and a large area of empty space. Done well they are calm, strong and memorable. Done badly they are just empty.

## What you need

Very little, which is part of the appeal.

- **A telephoto zoom,** to isolate a subject from busy surroundings. A 70-200 or 100-400 mm lets you crop the scene in camera from a distance
- **A wide lens,** for the opposite approach: a small subject in a big sky or a wide sea
- **Exposure compensation** you can set quickly. Bright, plain scenes fool the meter
- **A clean sensor and lens.** Plain skies show every speck of dust

## Starting settings

- **Mode:** Aperture priority, with exposure compensation ready to dial in
- **Aperture:** f/5.6 to f/8; smaller apertures show sensor dust in plain skies
- **Shutter speed:** 1/125 s to 1/500 s handheld; 1/500 s or faster at 400 mm
- **ISO:** 100 to 400, kept low so plain sky, fog and water stay smooth
- **Focal length:** 70 to 400 mm full frame to isolate; 16 to 24 mm for a small subject in big space
- **Exposure compensation:** +1 to +2 stops for snow, fog or sand; minus for dark scenes

These are starting points. Take a frame, check the histogram, and adjust one thing at a time.

## Negative space

The empty area around the subject is called negative space. It is not wasted. It gives the subject room, sets the mood and makes something small feel significant. A lone boat on a flat calm sea is more powerful than the same boat in a busy harbour.

Negative space does not have to be white or empty sky. A plain wall, deep shadow, a smooth slope of sand or flat water all count, as long as nothing in it competes with the subject.

## Where to find it

- Fog and mist, which erase the background
- Flat calm water and plain skies
- Snow, sand and beaches at low tide
- Long exposures, which smooth water and cloud into plain tone
- Plain walls in towns, with one person, door or window

## Isolating with a long lens

A telephoto is one of the easiest ways to simplify. Its narrow angle of view takes in only a small slice of background, so it is easier to find a plain one behind your subject. Move a few steps sideways and the background behind the subject changes completely.

## Keep it simple, but not empty

The subject still needs to be placed with care. Thirds work well, and so does putting a small subject low or to one side so the space reads as deliberate. Check every edge of the frame for a stray branch, post or bird that pulls the eye away.

Give the space a direction. A subject facing or moving into the space feels settled. The same subject facing out of the frame, with all the space behind it, feels uneasy. Either can work, but choose it.

There is no rule for how small the subject can be, but the sums help. In a 3:2 frame, a subject one tenth of the width and one third of the height covers 1/10 × 1/3 = 1/30 of the picture. That can still hold the frame, if it is the only thing with real contrast.

## Colour and tone

Limit the palette. Two or three tones, or one colour against neutral grey, works better than a full range. Many minimalist images are slightly overexposed to keep the space clean and light.

A single strong colour against muted surroundings becomes a subject on its own, even when it is small.

Black and white is another way to reduce a picture to tone. The Black and white lesson covers how to judge it.

## Exposing bright, plain scenes

The camera's meter assumes the scene averages out to mid grey. Point it at snow, fog, white sand or a bright sky and it darkens everything towards grey. Add exposure compensation to bring the brightness back.

Here is a worked example. The camera meters a misty beach at **f/8, 1/500 s, ISO 100**, and the result looks dull and grey.

1. Dial in +2 stops of exposure compensation
2. Each stop doubles the time, so 1/500 s becomes 1/250 s, then 1/125 s
3. Check the histogram. The graph should sit well to the right without pressing against the right edge
4. If the highlight warning flashes across the space, come back by a third or two thirds of a stop

Dark minimal scenes work the other way. A subject against deep shadow may need minus compensation to keep the shadow black rather than a muddy grey.

## The method in the field

1. Find the space first: fog, water, sky, sand, a plain wall
2. Look for one subject with clear contrast against it, in tone, colour or shape
3. Choose the lens: telephoto to cut out clutter, wide to make the space feel vast
4. Place the subject low, to one side or on a thirds point, with space in the direction it faces
5. Walk your eye round every edge and remove anything that does not belong, by moving, zooming or waiting
6. Set exposure compensation and check the histogram
7. Take one version with the subject smaller and one with it larger. The right size is rarely obvious at the scene

## Common mistakes

- **Empty, not minimal.** Space with no subject, or a subject too weak to hold it. Make sure one thing clearly stands out
- **Grey snow and grey fog.** The meter at work. Add exposure compensation
- **Clutter at the edges.** A half-visible post, a distant person, a strand of grass. Check all four edges and the corners
- **Dust spots.** Small apertures and plain skies show sensor dust clearly. Check at 100% in editing and remove the spots, or clean the sensor
- **A tilted horizon.** With nothing else in the frame, even a slight slope is obvious. Use the level
- **Banding.** Smooth gradients in sky or fog can break into visible steps when pushed hard in editing. Shoot RAW and edit gently

## Checking it in camera

Look at the playback image small, the size of a thumbnail. A strong minimalist picture still reads at that size: one clear shape and a clean space. Then zoom into each corner for stray detail, and check the histogram and the highlight warning.

> Keep taking things out until it stops working. Then put the last one back.
