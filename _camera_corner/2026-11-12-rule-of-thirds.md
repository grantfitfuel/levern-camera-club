Most beginners put the subject in the middle of the frame. It is the obvious place, and it is usually the dullest. The rule of thirds is the quickest fix.

## How it works

Imagine two lines across the frame and two lines down it, splitting it into nine equal parts. Place your subject on one of the four points where the lines cross, and run strong edges such as the horizon along one of the lines.

An off-centre subject creates tension. The eye has somewhere to go, and the empty part of the frame starts to matter.

The grid is a guide, not a target to hit to the pixel. A subject close to a crossing point does the same job as one exactly on it.

## What counts as the subject

The part you place is the part the eye goes to first. That is not always the whole object.

- **People and animals:** the eyes, or the whole face if the person is small in the frame
- **A tree or a building:** the trunk or the main mass, not the tips of the branches or the chimney
- **A small figure, boat or car in a big landscape:** the whole thing, because it is small enough to act as a point
- **Lines and edges:** the horizon, a shoreline or the edge of a shadow go along a line, not through a point

When there are two subjects, try them on opposite crossing points, such as top left and bottom right. The eye then travels between them across the whole frame.

## Use the grid in your camera

Almost every camera and phone can show a thirds grid on screen. Turn it on and leave it on. After a few weeks you will place things on the thirds without thinking.

The setting is usually in the display or shooting menu, under a name such as grid display or grid lines. Some cameras offer finer grids as well. The three by three grid is the one you want for this.

## Starting settings

- **Mode:** Aperture priority, so attention stays on placement rather than exposure
- **Aperture:** f/8 to f/11 for landscapes; f/2.8 to f/5.6 for portraits and street
- **Shutter speed:** 1/250 s to 1/500 s for people walking; at least one over the focal length otherwise
- **ISO:** ISO 100 to 400 in daylight; raise it in poor light rather than accept a blurred frame
- **Focus:** Single-point autofocus, with the point moved onto the subject instead of focusing and recomposing
- **Display:** Three by three grid turned on and left on

These are starting points. Take a frame, check the histogram, and adjust one thing at a time.

## The method in the field

1. Decide what the photograph is about before you lift the camera. If you cannot say it in a few words, keep looking
2. Turn on the grid and frame loosely, with the main subject roughly on a crossing point
3. Choose which crossing point. Put the subject on the side that leaves space where it is looking or moving
4. Run the horizon or the strongest line along the upper or lower third, not through the middle
5. Move your feet, not just the camera. One step to the side changes what sits behind the subject
6. Put the focus point on the subject itself. Focusing in the centre and then recomposing can shift the focus off the subject, especially at wide apertures
7. Check the edges for anything bright, distracting or cut in half, then take the picture
8. Take a second frame with the subject on the opposite third and a third frame with it centred. Compare them later on a bigger screen

## Give it room

If your subject is looking or moving, put the space in front of it, not behind. A walker heading left belongs on the right third, with the empty frame ahead of them.

Put the same walker on the left third, facing the left edge, and the picture feels cramped, as if they are about to walk out of it. Occasionally that tension is the point, for example a figure leaving a scene. Most of the time it just looks like a mistake.

The same applies to a face in profile, a car, a bird in flight or a boat under way. Give the direction of travel about two thirds of the frame.

## Worked scenarios

- **A lone tree on a hillside.** Put the trunk on the left or right vertical line and the skyline along the upper third. If the sky is plain grey, push the skyline higher still and let the slope fill the frame
- **A head-and-shoulders portrait.** Put the nearer eye on an upper crossing point. In an upright frame the eyes often land on the upper third line naturally if you leave a little space above the head
- **A boat on a loch.** The boat is small in the frame, so it is the point. Put it on a lower crossing point, run the far shore along the upper third, and let the water fill the rest
- **A busy street.** Choose the third first, then wait for someone to walk into it, instead of chasing people with the camera

## Cropping afterwards

The grid works in editing too. Lightroom's crop tool can lay a thirds grid over the picture while you crop.

It helps to know where the lines fall. In a frame 6000 pixels wide and 4000 tall, the vertical thirds sit at 2000 and 4000 pixels across (6000 ÷ 3 = 2000). The horizontal thirds sit at about 1333 and 2667 pixels down (4000 ÷ 3 ≈ 1333).

Change the shape and the grid moves with it. Crop the same frame to a 4000 by 4000 square and the vertical thirds now fall at about 1333 and 2667 pixels across the new frame. A subject that was on a third before may not be on one after, so check the grid again after every change of shape.

Cropping also costs detail. A crop that keeps two thirds of the width and two thirds of the height keeps 2/3 × 2/3 = 4/9 of the pixels, which is less than half. Get the placement close in camera and use the crop for fine adjustment.

## Checking your result

- Look at the picture small, as a thumbnail on the camera's playback screen. Placement and balance show up better small than full size
- Notice where your eye lands first. If it is a bright patch at the edge and not the subject, recompose or fix the edge
- Check the space in front of anything that is looking or moving
- Compare the off-centre frame with the centred one you took as a test. If the centred one is stronger, the scene probably wanted symmetry

## Common mistakes

- **Everything on a third.** Several things, each on its own crossing point, compete with each other. Choose one main subject and let the rest support it
- **The horizon on a third but the subject in the middle.** Place the main subject first, then the lines
- **Focusing and recomposing at a wide aperture.** The focus can slip off the eyes. Move the focus point instead of the camera
- **Room behind the subject instead of in front.** Swap sides
- **Trusting the grid over the picture.** If the frame feels wrong with the subject exactly on the point, nudge it until it feels right

## When to break it

- Symmetry and reflections usually want the centre
- A single small subject in a big empty space can sit almost anywhere
- Square frames often suit a central subject
- A face looking straight into the lens often has more force in the centre

> The rule of thirds is a starting point, not a law. Learn it well enough that breaking it is a choice.
