Most composition advice tells you to move the subject off-centre. Symmetry is the exception. When a scene is balanced, centring it is the strongest choice.

## Kinds of symmetry

- **Mirror symmetry:** one half reflects the other across a line. A reflection in still water mirrors top to bottom. A doorway or a church nave mirrors left to right
- **Radial symmetry:** shapes repeat around a centre point, like a stairwell seen from below, a rose window or a dome
- **Repetition:** the same shape again and again across the frame, such as windows, posts or creels

In each case, find the line or point the scene is balanced on and place it exactly.

## What you need

- **A tripod,** for precise placement. Moving a few millimetres is far easier on a tripod than by hand
- **The grid display and the electronic level** in your camera. Turn both on
- **Lens corrections.** Wide lenses can bow straight lines near the edges, which shows at once in a symmetrical frame. Lightroom's lens corrections remove most of it
- **A polariser,** used with care. It cuts glare, but at full strength it will also remove the reflection you came for

## Starting settings

- **Mode:** Aperture priority, on a tripod for precise placement
- **Aperture:** f/8 to f/11, for sharpness from edge to edge
- **Shutter speed:** Any speed on a tripod with the 2-second timer; 1/125 s or faster handheld
- **ISO:** 100 to 400 on a tripod; raise it only when handheld in dim interiors
- **Focal length:** 16 mm to 35 mm full frame for interiors and reflections; 70 mm to 200 mm for patterns
- **Exposure:** Expose for the brighter half of a reflection, keeping its highlights off the right edge

These are starting points. Take a frame, check the histogram, and adjust one thing at a time.

## Reflections

Still water is the easiest symmetry to find. Get to a loch early, before the wind gets up, put the horizon through the middle and let the reflection mirror the scene. A low angle, close to the water, makes the reflection larger.

A few things decide how good the reflection is:

- **Wind.** Even a light breeze breaks the surface. Calm water is more likely early in the morning and around sunset
- **Puddles.** After rain, a puddle a few centimetres across can reflect a whole building if you put the lens right down beside it
- **Brightness.** The reflection is usually a little darker than the scene above it. Expose for the brighter half, check the histogram, and lift the reflection slightly in editing if it needs it
- **The polariser.** Turn it while watching the screen. At one point the reflection fades and you see into the water. Turn it back until the reflection is at its strongest

## Built symmetry

Architecture is full of it: bridges, doorways, church interiors, corridors. Stand exactly in the middle, keep the camera level and square to the subject, and check the edges match. Being a few centimetres off-centre is very obvious in a symmetrical frame.

Symmetry in a building is usually designed in on purpose, so the centre line is there to be found: the middle of a door, the peak of a gable, a line of floor tiles.

## Keeping lines straight

When you tilt the camera up at a building, its vertical lines lean towards each other. In a symmetrical frame the lean looks like a mistake unless it is clearly the point.

- Keep the back of the camera vertical, using the level, and step back or use a wider focal length to fit the building in
- Crop away the extra foreground afterwards
- Or correct it in editing with a perspective tool, such as Transform in Lightroom

Looking straight up, at a stairwell or a dome, the opposite applies. There are no verticals to keep straight, so aim the lens directly at the centre and let everything converge on it.

## The field method

1. Find the line or point of symmetry: the centre of the arch, the axis of the corridor, the middle of the dome
2. Stand on that line. Use floor tiles, paving joints or a centre mark to place your feet
3. Set up the tripod and level the camera with the electronic level, both side to side and front to back
4. Turn on the grid and line the centre of the scene up with the centre of the frame
5. Compare matching features near the left and right edges. If one side shows more, move the whole tripod sideways. Panning the head instead turns the camera off square
6. Take the frame, then check it in playback before you move

## Patterns

Repetition is pleasing to the eye: rows of windows, ripples in sand, stacked lobster creels on a harbour wall. Fill the frame so the pattern seems to carry on beyond the edges.

- **Use a longer lens from further back.** At a distance, repeated objects near and far look more similar in size, instead of shrinking quickly into the distance. A 70-200 mm zoom suits this well
- **Choose square on or angled.** Square on gives a flat, graphic pattern. An angle gives a pattern that recedes. Both work. Something in between usually looks undecided
- **Look for low light.** Side light raises the texture in sand, stone and wood, and turns a flat surface into a pattern of light and shadow

## Break the pattern

The strongest pattern photographs often contain one thing that does not fit. A single red door in a terrace. One gull on a row of posts. The pattern sets up an expectation and the break becomes the subject.

The break works best when it is clearly different: a different colour, a different shape, or the only thing that moves. If the pattern is static, set up and wait for a person, a bird or a patch of light to supply it. Then place the break with care, either dead centre in a strict grid, or clearly off-centre so the regular pattern has room around it.

## Worked scenarios

- **A still loch at dawn.** Put the shoreline across the exact middle of the frame. Lower the tripod until the lens is close to the water, so the reflection is as large as possible. If a breeze ruffles the surface, wait for it to settle
- **A church nave.** Stand on the centre line of the aisle, level the camera, and use a wide focal length such as 16 mm to take in both rows of columns. Check that the end window sits in the middle and the first columns are the same distance from each edge
- **A harbour wall of creels.** Stand back with a telephoto and fill the frame edge to edge with the stack. Then look for the one creel in a different colour, or wait for a gull to land

## Common mistakes

- **Nearly symmetrical.** A frame that is almost centred looks like an error. Centre it exactly, or move clearly off-centre
- **A tilted horizon.** In a reflection the tilt shows twice. Use the level, and straighten in editing if needed
- **Bowed lines.** Wide-angle distortion curves straight edges near the sides of the frame. Apply lens corrections or zoom in slightly
- **Clutter on one side.** A bin, a sign or half a person on one edge breaks the balance. Move, wait or crop
- **A pattern that stops.** If the end of the pattern shows inside the frame, the eye goes straight to it. Zoom in or move closer until the pattern fills the frame

## Checking it in camera

Zoom into the playback image and compare matching points at the left and right edges, such as the bottom corners of a doorway. They should sit the same distance from the edge and at the same height. Then check the centre line and horizon against the grid. Do it before you leave. A small error is hard to fix later without cropping away part of the scene.

> Symmetry is calm. A broken pattern is a story.
