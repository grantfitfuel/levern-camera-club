A natural frame is anything in the scene that surrounds your subject: a doorway, branches, a window, the gap between two buildings. It adds depth, gives context and keeps the eye from drifting off the edges.

## Why it works

The frame creates layers. Something close, the subject further away, and the space between them make a flat image feel three-dimensional. It also shows where you were standing, which tells a story of its own.

It also does the job of the picture's edges. The eye tends to wander outwards. A frame inside the frame stops it and turns it back towards the subject.

## Where to look

- Doorways and arches in old towns and castles
- Overhanging branches at the edge of a wood
- Rock arches and caves on the coast
- Bridges, seen from underneath
- Windows, from inside looking out or from outside looking in
- Gaps between buildings, and the walls of a courtyard seen from below
- Fences, railings and gates
- Shadows, which can frame a lit patch of street

## Keep the frame quiet

The frame should support the subject, not compete with it. It works best when it is darker than the view, or simpler. A shadowy archway around a bright street is the classic example.

A busy frame, with bright patches, signs or texture as strong as the subject, pulls the eye back to the edges. If the frame is busy, make it darker, softer or smaller in the picture.

## Starting settings

- **Mode:** Aperture priority, with spot or centre-weighted metering on the view so the dark frame does not fool it
- **Aperture:** f/11 to f/16 to keep frame and view sharp; around f/2.8 to melt the frame into a soft border
- **Shutter speed:** 1/125 s to 1/500 s handheld in daylight; slower on a tripod when stopped down in shade
- **ISO:** 100 to 400 in daylight; raise it only if the shutter speed gets too slow to hold
- **Focal length:** 24 mm to 35 mm full frame for arches and doorways; step back to fit the frame in
- **Focus:** About 1.8 m at 24 mm and f/11 for both sharp; otherwise on the subject

These are starting points. Take a frame, check the histogram, and adjust one thing at a time.

## Exposure

When the frame is in shade and the view is in light, the camera cannot always hold detail in both. Decide which matters.

- **Expose for the view** and let the frame fall dark, even to silhouette. This is usually the stronger picture, and it keeps the frame quiet
- **Use spot or centre-weighted metering** on the view, so the dark frame does not fool the meter into brightening everything
- **Check the histogram.** The dark frame will sit on the left. What matters is that the view is not pushed against the right edge
- **If you need detail in both,** take two frames, one for each, and blend them in editing

## Focus and depth

Decide what should be sharp. At f/11 or f/16 both the frame and the view stay crisp. At f/2.8 the frame melts into a soft border and the subject stands out. Both are valid choices.

Here is a worked example. You stand with an archway 1 metre away and a view in the distance, using a 24 mm lens on a full-frame camera.

1. At f/11 the hyperfocal distance is about 1.8 metres. The sum is 24 × 24 = 576, divided by 11 × 0.03 = 0.33, which gives about 1745 mm, plus the 24 mm focal length, so roughly 1.77 metres
2. Focus at about 1.8 metres and everything from about half that distance, 0.9 metres, to the far distance is acceptably sharp. The arch at 1 metre is just inside
3. At f/2.8 the hyperfocal distance is about 6.9 metres (576 ÷ 0.084 ≈ 6857 mm, plus 24 mm). Focus on the view 20 metres away and sharpness only starts at about 5 metres, so the arch at 1 metre becomes a soft border

The 0.03 mm in the sum is the usual allowance for full frame. Phone apps will do the sum for you, but knowing it tells you why stepping back from the frame makes it easier to keep sharp.

## The method in the field

1. Find the subject first, then look for something near you that could surround it
2. Move until the frame sits around the subject, not across it. Small steps sideways or up and down change the shape a great deal
3. Decide whether the frame should be sharp or soft, and set the aperture to match
4. Focus on the subject, or at the hyperfocal distance if you want both sharp
5. Meter for the view and let the frame go darker
6. Check that the frame does not touch or cut into the subject. Leave a gap between them
7. Check the edges of the whole picture for anything bright creeping in around the frame
8. Take a version without the frame as well, to see what it adds

## Partial frames and edges

A frame does not need four sides. A single branch across the top, or a wall down one side, is enough to close off that edge and hold the eye in.

## Worked scenarios

- **A castle through an archway.** Stand back far enough that the arch fits inside the frame with some wall around it. Meter for the castle and let the arch go dark
- **A loch through branches.** Use f/2.8 and get close to the leaves, so they become a soft border across the top
- **A street seen through a window.** Press the lens against the glass to cut out reflections, and keep the window edges straight
- **A figure in a doorway.** The doorway is the frame and the figure is the subject. Wait until they step into the lit part

## Checking in camera

1. Zoom in on the subject and check it is sharp, unless you chose to keep the frame sharp and the view soft
2. Run your eye round the edges of the frame for bright gaps or stray objects that break it
3. Check the histogram for the view, not the frame. A dark arch can push the meter into overexposing the view beyond it
4. Look at the thumbnail. The frame should lead to the subject, not compete with it

## Common mistakes

- **A frame brighter than the view.** The eye goes to the edges. Change position, or expose so the frame darkens
- **The frame touching the subject.** A branch that meets the top of a tower merges with it. Move until there is clear space between them
- **A frame cut off awkwardly.** If half an arch sits at the edge, either include more of it or leave it out
- **Too much frame.** If the frame fills most of the picture, the subject becomes small and lost. Step forward
- **The wrong thing in focus.** Decide before you shoot whether the frame or the view should be sharp

> A frame does not have to be complete. Two branches can be enough.
