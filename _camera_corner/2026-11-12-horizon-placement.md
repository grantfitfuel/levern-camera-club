The horizon is usually the strongest line in a landscape. Where you put it tells the viewer what the photograph is about before they have looked at anything else.

## Read the scene first

Before you place the horizon, decide which half has more to say. Look at the sky: texture, colour, movement, light breaking through. Then look at the land: rocks, flowers, a path, patterns in sand or water.

Whichever half carries the picture gets the room. If you cannot choose, that is often a sign the scene is a reflection, or that the horizon should not be in the frame at all.

## Starting settings

- **Mode:** Aperture priority
- **Aperture:** f/8 to f/11, so the foreground and the horizon are both sharp
- **Shutter speed:** 1/125 s to 1/500 s handheld; slower is fine on a tripod in dawn light
- **ISO:** Lowest native ISO, usually 100; raise it only if the handheld shutter speed drops too low
- **Focal length:** 16 mm to 100 mm full frame, depending on how much of the scene you want
- **Display:** Grid and electronic level switched on

These are starting points. Take a frame, check the histogram, and adjust one thing at a time.

## Low horizon: sky-led

Put the horizon on the lower third when the sky is doing something worth showing: storm cloud, a sunset, a big clearing after rain. The land becomes a thin base for the drama above it.

A low horizon still needs something on the land. A small, clear shape against the sky, such as a tree, a building or a figure, stops the base being just a dark strip.

## High horizon: land-led

Put it on the upper third when the interest is in front of you: rocks on the shore, patterns in the sand, a path leading in. On a flat grey day this is often the only way to make a landscape work, because it leaves the dull sky out.

## Centred: for reflections

A horizon through the middle splits the frame in two equal halves. That usually feels static, which is exactly why it suits a mirror-calm loch. The reflection and the scene balance each other.

If you centre it, centre it exactly. A horizon slightly off the middle looks like a mistake, not a choice.

Calm water is most likely early in the morning, before the wind gets up. If you use a polariser, turn it while watching the reflection: at one angle it can remove the reflection you came for.

## Where the camera puts the horizon

With the camera held level, a distant horizon falls across the middle of the frame. To move it to the lower third you tilt the camera up. To move it to the upper third you tilt it down.

How far you tilt depends on the lens. A full-frame sensor is 24 mm tall in landscape format. Moving the horizon from the centre to a third line moves it one sixth of the frame height, which is 24 ÷ 6 = 4 mm on the sensor.

- **At 16 mm:** the tilt is about 14 degrees, because 4 ÷ 16 = 0.25, and the angle whose tangent is 0.25 is about 14 degrees
- **At 100 mm:** the tilt is only about 2.3 degrees, because 4 ÷ 100 = 0.04

That is why tilting causes problems with a wide lens and hardly any with a long one. Tilting up makes trees and buildings near the edges lean inwards. Tilting down makes them lean outwards. If that matters, keep the camera level and crop, step back and use a longer focal length, or straighten the lean afterwards.

## Keep it level

A sloping horizon is the most common mistake in landscape photography, and the easiest to fix. Turn on the level in your camera, or straighten it afterwards. On the coast, a tilted sea looks as if it is draining out of the picture.

- **The electronic level** in the viewfinder or on the screen is the quickest check. Many cameras show tilt forward and back as well as side to side
- **A level tripod is not a level camera.** The head sits on top of the legs, so check the camera itself
- **The sea is the only horizon you can trust.** Hills and fields slope naturally. On land, check a vertical such as a building or a fence post instead
- **In Lightroom,** the crop tool has a straighten option. Drag it along the horizon and the picture rotates to match. Rotating trims the corners, so leave a little room at the edges when you frame

## Wide lenses and curved horizons

Many wide lenses bend straight lines that do not pass through the centre of the frame. A horizon near the top or bottom edge can bow into a slight curve. A horizon through the centre stays straight. Lens profile corrections in Lightroom remove most of this, so turn them on before you judge whether the horizon is level.

## The method in the field

1. Decide whether the photograph is about the land, the sky or a reflection
2. Turn on the grid and the electronic level
3. Place the horizon on the lower third, the upper third or dead centre to match
4. Check the edges for trees or buildings leaning because of tilt, and step back with a longer lens if they distract
5. Check that the horizon does not cut through a head, the top of a tree or the peak of a hill
6. Check the exposure. The sky is usually much brighter than the land, so look at the histogram and the highlight warning
7. Take the frame, then take the other two placements for comparison

## Exposure and the horizon

The half you give the most room to is the half you expose for. With a low horizon, protect the sky and let the land go darker. With a high horizon, expose for the land and accept a brighter sky, or leave the sky out.

If you need detail in both, a graduated ND filter darkens the sky without touching the land. The alternative is two frames, one for each, blended in editing.

## Worked scenarios

- **A storm moving over a beach.** Horizon on the lower third, exposure set for the cloud, a figure or a rock on the sand for scale
- **A flat grey day on a rocky shore.** Horizon on the upper third, or point the camera down and leave it out altogether
- **A calm loch at dawn.** Horizon dead centre, polariser turned to keep the reflection
- **A sunset behind a dark ridge.** The ridge line is your horizon. Put it on the lower third and let it fall to silhouette

## Checking in camera

1. Check the horizon against the electronic level or the grid, not against the edge of the screen
2. Look at the frame small, as a thumbnail, and ask whether the bigger share of the frame went to the more interesting part
3. Check the histogram. A bright sky over dark land often touches both edges at once. Bracket, or wait for softer light
4. With a wide lens, look along the horizon near the edges of the frame for any bend

## Common mistakes

- **A sloping horizon.** Use the level, or straighten in editing
- **Centring by habit.** Without a reflection, a centred horizon usually feels undecided. Move it to a third
- **A horizon through a subject.** A line cutting through someone's neck or across the top of a tree draws the eye to the wrong place. Raise or lower the camera
- **Leaning edges from a tilted wide lens.** Level the camera and crop, or use a longer lens
- **Levelling by a hillside.** Land slopes. Use a vertical or the camera's level

> Decide what the photograph is about, then give it the most room.
