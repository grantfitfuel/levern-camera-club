The eye follows lines. Put a line in the frame that runs towards your subject and you have done half the work of telling people where to look.

## Where to find them

- Roads and paths running away from you
- Dry stone walls and fences crossing a hillside
- Rivers, burns and the curve of a shoreline
- Piers, railings and rows of windows in town
- Tide lines, ripples in sand and the edge of the surf
- Shadows, especially late in the day when they are long
- Staircases, handrails and the joins in a pavement
- Light trails from traffic at night

Lines do not have to be drawn. A row of lamp posts, a line of boats or a person's gaze all act as lines, because the eye joins the dots.

## Starting settings

- **Mode:** Aperture priority
- **Aperture:** f/8 to f/11 with a wide lens, so the line stays sharp from front to back
- **Shutter speed:** 1/125 s to 1/500 s handheld; the faster end with a long lens
- **ISO:** 100 to 400 in daylight; raise it in poor light rather than slowing the shutter
- **Focal length:** 16 to 24 mm full frame to sweep lines in; 100 to 200 mm to stack them
- **Focus:** About a third of the way into the scene; check the line is sharp where it starts

These are starting points. Take a frame, check the histogram, and adjust one thing at a time.

## What different lines do

- **Diagonals** feel active. They create movement and depth, and they are the strongest leading lines
- **Converging lines** run away from you and meet in the distance. They pull the eye into the frame and make it feel deep
- **Horizontal lines** feel calm and stable. They lead across the frame, not into it
- **Vertical lines** feel strong and upright. They lead the eye up, which suits trees and buildings

## Make them lead somewhere

A line that runs out of the side of the frame takes the eye with it. The strongest lines start near a corner or the bottom edge and end at something worth looking at: a building, a peak, a figure.

The end of the line is the point of the picture. If the line leads to nothing, the viewer arrives and finds an empty space. Look for a clear subject where the line ends or where several lines meet.

## Get low and go wide

A wide-angle lens close to the ground stretches lines out and makes them converge faster. A wall that looks ordinary at head height can sweep right through the frame from knee height.

Strictly, it is your position that changes the line, not the lens. Getting close to the start of the line makes the near part look large and the far part look small. The wide lens is what lets you stand that close and still fit the whole line in.

## Long lenses and stacked lines

A long lens does the opposite. From far away the line is seen almost side on, so it looks flatter and the parts of it stack up. Dune ridges, rows of hills and layers of streets work this way. The lines stop pulling you in and start to form layers and pattern instead.

## Curves

Straight lines are direct and strong. Curves, like an S-bend in a river or a road winding through a glen, slow the eye down and make it wander. Both work. Choose the one that matches the mood.

An S-curve works best when it starts near a bottom corner and winds up through the frame. Higher ground helps: from above you see more of the bend, and from ground level it can flatten into a straight strip.

## Lines in buildings

Looking up at a building makes its vertical lines lean in towards the top of the frame. That can be a strong leading line in its own right, pulling the eye up to the roof or the sky.

If you plan to straighten, leave room around the building when you frame. The correction stretches the top of the picture and crops the edges.

## The method in the field

1. Find the subject, or a place where a subject will appear
2. Look for a line that runs towards it: a path, a wall, a shoreline, a shadow
3. Walk until the line starts near a corner or the bottom edge of the frame and ends at the subject
4. Try lower. Crouch or put the camera near the ground and see whether the line becomes stronger
5. Choose the lens. Wide and close to make the line sweep in, long and further back to stack lines into layers
6. Set an aperture that keeps the line sharp from front to back if it is the main structure, often f/8 to f/11 with a wide lens
7. Check that the line does not run out of the side of the frame, then take the picture
8. Take one frame from head height as well, to compare

## Worked scenarios

- **A path through a field to a tree.** Stand on the path, camera low, so the path enters from the bottom edge and ends at the trunk. Put the tree on a third
- **A pier at sunset.** Stand at one side so the pier runs in diagonally from a bottom corner, not straight up the middle
- **A wall on a hillside.** Find where the wall starts close to you and climbs towards a peak or a cottage
- **A city street.** Stand in the middle of a pavement or crossing (safely) and let the kerbs and building fronts converge on a figure or a landmark

## Common mistakes

- **A line that leads nowhere.** Find a subject at the end, or wait for one to arrive
- **A line that leaves the frame.** Move until it ends inside the picture
- **A line straight up the middle.** Often static. Step sideways and let it enter from a corner
- **Too many lines.** Several lines pulling in different directions confuse the eye. Simplify the frame
- **A soft foreground.** If the line starts close to the lens, make sure it is sharp where it starts

## Checking your result

- Follow the line on the screen with your eye. Where you end up is where the viewer ends up
- Check the bottom corners. Leading lines usually start there, and clutter there blocks the way in
- Compare the low frame with the head-height frame and keep the one that pulls harder

> Find the line first. Then walk until it points where you want.
