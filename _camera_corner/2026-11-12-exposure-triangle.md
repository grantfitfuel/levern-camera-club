Every exposure comes down to three settings. Each one lets in more or less light, and each one has a side effect you can see in the picture.

## Aperture

The aperture is the opening in the lens, written as an f-number. **A smaller number means a bigger opening**, so f/2.8 lets in far more light than f/16.

The full stops run f/1.4, f/2, f/2.8, f/4, f/5.6, f/8, f/11, f/16 and f/22. Each step along that list halves the light. The numbers look odd because each one is the one before multiplied by about 1.4, the square root of two.

It also controls depth of field. Wide open, only your subject is sharp and the background melts away. Stopped down to f/11 or f/16, everything from the foreground rocks to the far hills stays sharp, which is why most landscape work sits there.

Go much past f/16 and the whole picture starts to soften slightly. This is diffraction, and it is why f/22 is a last resort rather than a default.

## Shutter speed

How long the sensor is exposed. 1/1000 s freezes a wave mid-break. A full second turns moving water into mist.

The full stops run 1/1000, 1/500, 1/250, 1/125, 1/60, 1/30, 1/15, 1/8, 1/4, 1/2 and 1 second. Some of the numbers are rounded, but each step is a doubling.

The catch is camera shake. A rough rule for handheld shooting: keep the shutter at least as fast as one over your focal length, so 1/50 s on a 50 mm lens and 1/200 s at 200 mm. Any slower and you need a tripod. On a camera with a smaller, crop sensor, multiply the focal length by the crop factor first.

Image stabilisation lets you hold the camera at slower speeds, but it only deals with your movement. A person walking through the frame still blurs.

## ISO

How strongly the camera amplifies the signal. Doubling the ISO brightens the image by one stop, but adds grain and loses detail in the shadows. Keep it as low as the light allows.

The full stops run ISO 100, 200, 400, 800, 1600, 3200 and 6400. Do not be so wary of ISO that you accept a blurred picture. Some noise can be cleaned up in editing. Camera shake cannot.

## Stops

The three are linked by **stops**. One stop is a doubling or halving of the light. Going from f/8 to f/5.6, from 1/125 s to 1/60 s, or from ISO 400 to 800 each adds one stop.

> Change one setting by a stop and you can balance it by moving another the opposite way.

That trade is the whole game. f/8 at 1/125 s and f/5.6 at 1/250 s give the same brightness. The difference is what you want sharp and what you want frozen.

Most cameras move in thirds of a stop. Between f/8 and f/11 you will find f/9 and f/10. Between 1/125 s and 1/250 s you will find 1/160 s and 1/200 s. Between ISO 100 and 200 you will find 125 and 160. Three clicks make one stop.

## Starting settings

- **Mode:** Manual, so every change is yours and you see its effect straight away
- **Aperture:** f/8 to f/11 for general scenes; open to f/2.8 to f/4 for a soft background
- **Shutter speed:** 1/125 s to 1/250 s handheld, and never slower than one over the focal length
- **ISO:** Lowest native ISO, usually 100. Raise it only when the shutter would be too slow to hold
- **Metering:** Set the meter to zero, then add 1 to 2 stops for snow or take away 1 for dark scenes

These are starting points. Take a frame, check the histogram, and adjust one thing at a time.

## What the meter gets wrong

The camera's meter suggests settings that will make the scene an average, mid-grey brightness. Most of the time that works. It fails when the scene is not average.

- **Snow, white sand or a pale sky** filling the frame get darkened to grey. Add 1 to 2 stops
- **A dark subject on a dark background** gets brightened to grey. Take away about 1 stop
- **Strong backlight** makes the meter darken the picture, and the subject in front goes too dark. Add exposure for the subject

In aperture priority or shutter priority, make these corrections with exposure compensation. In manual, set the exposure so the meter reads above or below zero by the amount you want.

## Worked examples

Each example starts from a meter reading and moves settings by whole stops. Count the stops on each side. They should match.

1. **A portrait with a soft background.** The meter reads f/8, 1/125 s, ISO 100. Opening up to f/2.8 is three stops (f/8 to f/5.6 to f/4 to f/2.8), so the shutter must be three stops faster: 1/125 to 1/250 to 1/500 to 1/1000 s
2. **Indoors, handheld at 50 mm.** The meter reads f/4, 1/15 s, ISO 100, which is too slow to hold. Speed the shutter up two stops to 1/60 s (1/15 to 1/30 to 1/60), then raise the ISO two stops (100 to 200 to 400) to keep the brightness
3. **Moving water at 1/2 s.** The meter reads f/8, 1/30 s, ISO 100. Getting to 1/2 s takes four stops: 1/30 to 1/15 to 1/8 to 1/4 to 1/2. Closing down to f/22 gives only three (f/8 to f/11 to f/16 to f/22), so you land at 1/4 s. The last stop has to come from a filter, such as a polariser or an ND
4. **Sunny 16, with no meter at all.** In bright sun, f/16 with a shutter speed of one over the ISO gives a correct exposure. At ISO 100 that is f/16 at 1/100 s. Trade from there: f/8 is two stops wider, so the shutter goes two stops faster, 1/100 to 1/200 to 1/400 s

## Learning it in the field

1. Set the camera to manual and the ISO to its lowest native setting, usually 100
2. Decide what matters most for this picture: depth of field, movement, or a clean file. Set that control first
3. Set the second control from the light. If you chose the aperture, turn the shutter speed until the meter reads zero
4. Check the shutter speed against the handheld rule. If it is too slow, raise the ISO until it is fast enough, or use a tripod
5. Take the picture and check the histogram
6. Correct in whole stops first, then fine tune in thirds
7. Move one setting a stop, balance it with another, and take the same frame again to see what changed

## Checking your result in camera

The screen on the back is a poor judge of brightness, especially in bright sun or in the dark. Use the histogram.

- A graph piled against the right edge means lost highlights. Bring the exposure down
- A graph piled against the left edge means lost shadows. Bring it up, unless you want a silhouette
- Turn on highlight warnings. Areas that blink have no detail left
- Zoom in on the subject to check focus and camera shake. A picture can look sharp on a small screen and soft on a large one

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving the ISO high.** After a dark interior, the camera is still at ISO 3200 the next morning in sun. Check it every time you start
- **Blaming the lens for blur.** If everything is soft, the shutter was too slow. If only some of it is soft, look at depth of field and focus
- **Stopping down for everything.** f/22 loses sharpness to diffraction. Use f/8 to f/11 unless you need the extra depth
- **Trusting the meter on snow and sand.** It turns white to grey. Add exposure
- **Changing two settings at once while learning.** Change one, see what it does, then balance it

## My one tip

I shoot in full manual nearly all the time. Don't be afraid of it. Out in the field, there is no better way to learn how the exposure triangle works. Every change you make, you see straight away.
