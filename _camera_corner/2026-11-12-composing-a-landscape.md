Stand in front of a great view, take a picture, and it often looks smaller and emptier than it felt. The scene needs structure.

## What you need

- **A wide zoom** for big foregrounds, such as a 16-35 mm, and **a telephoto zoom** for picking out part of a view
- **A tripod,** so you can refine the composition slowly and use a small aperture in low light
- **A polariser,** to cut glare on wet rock and leaves and deepen a blue sky. It works most strongly when the sun is at right angles to the direction the lens points
- **The grid display and electronic level** turned on in the camera

## Starting settings

- **Mode:** Aperture priority, with the camera on a tripod
- **Aperture:** f/8 to f/11; avoid f/16 and f/22, where diffraction softens fine detail
- **Shutter speed:** 1/250 s down to several seconds on the tripod; slow speeds are fine in low light
- **ISO:** Lowest native ISO, usually 100
- **Focal length:** 16 mm to 35 mm full frame for foregrounds; around 135 mm to stack distant layers
- **Focus:** Hyperfocal at f/11: about 0.8 m at 16 mm, about 1.77 m at 24 mm
- **Filter:** Polariser for glare on wet rock and leaves, strongest with the sun at right angles

These are starting points. Take a frame, check the histogram, and adjust one thing at a time.

## Find a foreground

Get low and put something close to the lens: rocks, heather, a fence post, ripples on the shore. It gives depth and a starting point for the eye. Use a smaller aperture such as f/11 so it stays sharp along with the distance.

A foreground works best when it points into the scene or echoes something in it: rocks that lead towards a headland, a stream that runs towards the mountain.

## Keeping it all sharp

f/11 on its own does not make everything sharp. Where you focus matters as much.

The hyperfocal distance is the closest point you can focus on and still keep the far distance acceptably sharp. Focus there, and everything from about half that distance to the horizon looks sharp. The formula is the focal length squared, divided by the aperture number times the circle of confusion, plus the focal length. For a full-frame camera the circle of confusion is usually taken as 0.03 mm.

Here is a worked example at 16 mm and f/11.

1. 16 × 16 = 256
2. 11 × 0.03 = 0.33
3. 256 ÷ 0.33 = about 776 mm. Add the 16 mm and you get about 792 mm, so about 0.8 metres
4. Focus at 0.8 metres and everything from about 0.4 metres to the horizon is acceptably sharp

At 24 mm and f/11 the same sum gives 576 ÷ 0.33 = about 1745 mm, plus 24 mm is about 1.77 metres. The nearest sharp point is then about 0.9 metres away, so a foreground closer than that will be soft.

"Acceptably sharp" is judged at normal viewing sizes. For large prints, treat these distances as a minimum and check the foreground in playback.

Stopping down further is not a free fix. On a high-resolution sensor, diffraction starts to soften fine detail beyond about f/11, so f/16 and f/22 trade overall crispness for extra depth.

## Use lines

Roads, rivers, walls and shorelines lead the eye through the frame. Diagonals feel more dynamic than lines running straight across.

Lines work best when they start near the bottom edge or a bottom corner and lead towards the main subject. A line that runs out of the side of the frame takes the eye out with it.

## Foreground, middle and background

Strong landscapes usually have something at each depth: a foreground to step into, a middle ground to cross and a background to finish on. If one layer is empty, the picture can feel flat or unfinished.

A telephoto does the opposite job to a wide lens. Standing further back, it compresses distance, so the layers stack up and distant hills, dunes or ridges look closer together than they are. That suits views where the shapes, rather than the space between them, are the subject.

## Place the horizon on purpose

The rule of thirds is a useful starting point, not a law. If the sky is the story, give it two thirds of the frame. If it is a dull grey, give it a sliver or leave it out. Either way, keep the horizon level.

Avoid the exact middle unless you want symmetry, such as a reflection. In most landscapes a central horizon splits the picture into two halves competing for attention.

A sea horizon near the top or bottom of a wide-angle frame can bow from lens distortion. Lens corrections in Lightroom fix most of it.

## Light and timing

Composition and light work together. Low side light, early and late in the day, picks out the shape of the land and gives texture to rock, grass and sand. Light from high overhead flattens it.

Use the planner to find when the sun will be low, then arrive early enough to settle on a composition before the light arrives. That is when the same view gives a different picture every few minutes, so keep looking rather than taking one frame and leaving.

## The method in the field

1. Walk the location before setting up. Look at the view from several heights and positions, not only the first spot you reach
2. Decide what drew you in. That is your subject
3. Find a foreground that leads towards it, and a line that connects the two
4. Set the tripod low, compose and level the camera
5. Place the horizon deliberately: high for a strong foreground, low for a strong sky
6. Set f/11 and focus at the hyperfocal distance for your focal length. If the foreground is closer than half that distance, plan to focus stack
7. Walk your eye round the edges of the frame, then take the shot
8. Check it in playback before you move on

## Simplify

Most weak compositions have too much in them. Before you press the shutter, walk your eye round the edges of the frame and ask what each thing is doing there.

- Move a few steps to separate overlapping shapes
- Zoom in on the part of the view that drew you in
- Try a portrait frame as well as a landscape one

## Common mistakes

- **No foreground,** so the picture has no depth. Get lower and closer to something
- **A foreground with no link** to the background. Look for one that points into the scene
- **A soft foreground.** Focusing on the horizon with a wide lens often leaves close rocks soft. Focus nearer, as in the worked example
- **A sloping horizon.** Use the electronic level, and straighten in editing if needed
- **Mergers.** A tree growing out of a hilltop, or a post touching the horizon. Move until the shapes separate
- **A blown sky.** If the histogram is pressed against the right edge, reduce the exposure slightly, or take a second, darker frame for the sky and blend the two

## Checking it in camera

Magnify the playback image twice: once on the nearest part of the foreground and once on the horizon. Both should look crisp. Then look at the whole frame small, which shows the balance of light and dark more clearly than looking at it full size. Finally, check the histogram is not pressed hard against either edge.

> If it does not add to the picture, it takes away from it.
