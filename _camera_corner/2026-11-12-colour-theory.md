Colour is often the first thing someone notices about a photograph, before the subject and long before the composition. A little colour theory helps you see why some scenes feel calm, some feel energetic, and some just feel wrong.

## The colour wheel

Picture the colours of a rainbow bent round into a circle: red, orange, yellow, green, blue and violet, then back to red. That is the colour wheel, and nearly everything in colour theory is about where colours sit on it.

- **Primary colours** in light are red, green and blue. Your camera sensor and your screen both build every colour out of these three
- **Warm colours** run from red through orange to yellow
- **Cool colours** run from green through blue to violet

## Four harmonies worth knowing

**Complementary.** Two colours directly opposite each other, such as orange and blue, or red and green. They give the strongest contrast and make each other look more intense. A sunset over blue water, or a red door on a green hedge, is complementary.

**Analogous.** Three or so colours sitting side by side, such as yellow, orange and red in an autumn woodland. They feel calm and unified, because nothing fights.

**Triadic.** Three colours evenly spaced round the wheel, such as red, yellow and blue. Bold and playful, and easy to overdo. Often one colour leads and the other two support.

**Monochromatic.** Shades of a single colour, from dark to light. A blue-hour cityscape that is all blues, or a misty morning that is all soft greys and greens.

> When a scene feels busy, it is often because too many colours are competing. Simplify the colours and the picture usually simplifies with them.

## Warm comes forward, cool sits back

Warm colours appear to advance towards us and cool colours appear to recede. A warm subject against a cool background stands out naturally, which is why a person in a red jacket pops against a blue sky.

You can use that to create depth in landscapes: warm foreground rocks lit by low sun, with cool blue hills fading behind.

## Saturation and brightness matter too

A colour is not just its position on the wheel. It also has:

- **Saturation**, how strong or pure it is, from vivid to almost grey
- **Brightness**, how light or dark it is

Muted, low-saturation colours feel quiet and nostalgic. Highly saturated colours feel loud and energetic. Pushing saturation too far in editing is one of the quickest ways to make a photograph look cheap.

## The accent colour

One small patch of strong colour in an otherwise muted scene pulls the eye instantly. A red umbrella on a grey street, a yellow boat in a grey harbour. If you find one, build the picture around it and keep everything else quiet.

## Putting it into practice

1. Before you press the shutter, name the two or three main colours in the frame
2. Ask whether they work together: complementary, analogous or one accent
3. If something distracts, move, change your angle or zoom in until it is gone
4. In editing, adjust the colours you want rather than raising saturation across the whole picture

Later on the path, the white balance lesson shows how to make sure those colours come out the way you saw them.
